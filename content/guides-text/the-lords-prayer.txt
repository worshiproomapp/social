The Lord's Prayer: words and meaning
The Lord's Prayer: words and meaning
By the Worship Room team · November 10, 2026
The Lord's Prayer is the prayer Jesus taught His disciples in Matthew 6:9–13 and Luke 11:2–4. It begins "Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy" and moves from praise, to God's Kingdom and will, to our daily needs, forgiveness, and protection. Below are the full words and a line-by-line explanation.

The words of the Lord's Prayer
“Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy. Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven. Give us today our daily bread. Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors. Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one. For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen.”
Matthew 6:9–13
The closing line ("For yours is the Kingdom…") isn't found in some of the earliest manuscripts of Matthew, and Luke's shorter version doesn't include it. Many churches pray it as a fitting ending of praise.
What each line means
"Our Father in heaven"
Jesus invites us to approach God as a loving Father, close and personal, while remembering He is in heaven, above all things. And it's "our" Father: we never pray alone.
"May your name be kept holy"
To hallow God's name is to honor Him as He really is. Prayer starts with who God is before it turns to what we need.
"Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven."
We ask for God's good rule to show up here and now: in the world, in our communities, and in our own hearts. It's also a prayer of surrender: His will, not mine.
"Give us today our daily bread"
A request for what we need today, not a lifetime supply. It echoes manna in the wilderness, given one day at a time, and teaches daily dependence on God.
"Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors"
We receive God's forgiveness, and we extend it to others. Jesus ties the two closely together right after the prayer (Matthew 6:14–15).
"Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one"
An honest admission that we're weak and need God's protection, both from temptation and from evil itself.
"For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen."
The prayer ends where it began: with God's greatness. "Amen" means "so be it" or "truly."
How to pray the Lord's Prayer today
Pray it slowly. Pause after each line and let it sink in.
Use it as an outline. After each line, add your own words. After "daily bread," name what you need today. After "forgive us," name what you need forgiveness for, and who you need to forgive.
Pray it with others. It was given in the plural ("our," "us"), and believers around the world pray it together every week.
Jesus gave this prayer right after warning against "vain repetitions" (Matthew 6:7). The point isn't to recite it mechanically, but to let it shape how you pray.
Frequently asked questions
Where is the Lord's Prayer in the Bible?
The Lord's Prayer appears in Matthew 6:9–13, in the Sermon on the Mount, and in a shorter form in Luke 11:2–4, where a disciple asks Jesus, "Lord, teach us to pray."
Why is it called the Lord's Prayer?
It's called the Lord's Prayer because the Lord Jesus taught it to His disciples as a model for how to pray. It's also called the Our Father, after its opening words.
What is the difference between "debts" and "trespasses"?
Matthew's Gospel uses the word for "debts," and Luke uses "sins." Some churches say "trespasses," a traditional English wording drawn from the verses that follow in Matthew 6:14–15. All three point to the same idea: the wrongs we need forgiven and the wrongs we forgive.
