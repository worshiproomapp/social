Psalm 23 meaning, verse by verse
Psalm 23 meaning, verse by verse
By the Worship Room team · November 10, 2026
Psalm 23 is a psalm of David that pictures God as a shepherd who provides, leads, protects, and stays with His people through every season, including "the valley of the shadow of death." It ends with a host welcoming a guest to His table and a promise to dwell in God's house forever. Its verses held the top six spots on Bible Gateway's list of most popular Scriptures in 2025.

Psalm 23
1Yahweh is my shepherd; I shall lack nothing. 2He makes me lie down in green pastures. He leads me beside still waters. 3He restores my soul. He guides me in the paths of righteousness for his name’s sake. 4Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for you are with me. Your rod and your staff, they comfort me. 5You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies. You anoint my head with oil. My cup runs over. 6Surely goodness and loving kindness shall follow me all the days of my life, and I will dwell in Yahweh’s house forever.
Psalm 23:1–6
Verse-by-verse meaning
"Yahweh is my shepherd; I shall lack nothing" (verse 1)
David was a shepherd before he was a king, so he knew what sheep need: food, water, rest, protection, and someone to lead them. Calling God "my shepherd" is personal. The result is contentment: with this Shepherd, nothing essential is missing.
Green pastures and still waters (verses 2–3)
Sheep won't lie down when they're hungry or afraid, and they drink best from calm water. The Shepherd provides rest and refreshment, then "restores my soul," bringing back what's been worn down. He leads "in the paths of righteousness for his name's sake": the right paths, for the sake of His own good reputation.
The valley of the shadow of death (verse 4)
The psalm doesn't promise there will be no dark valleys. It promises you won't walk them alone. Notice the shift here: David stops talking about God ("he") and starts talking to Him ("you are with me"). In the darkest place, the relationship becomes closest. The rod protected the sheep from predators; the staff guided and rescued them.
A table prepared for you (verse 5)
The picture changes from shepherd to host. In the ancient world, a host who welcomed you to his table took responsibility for your safety. God honors His guest even while enemies look on. Anointing the head with oil was a sign of welcome and blessing, and an overflowing cup means more than enough.
Goodness and loving kindness (verse 6)
"Follow" is a strong word, closer to "pursue." God's goodness and steadfast love chase after His people every day. The psalm ends at home: dwelling in God's house, in His presence, forever.
Why Psalm 23 is read at funerals
Psalm 23 faces death honestly and answers it with presence and hope: "I will fear no evil, for you are with me," and "I will dwell in Yahweh's house forever." For Christians, it also echoes Jesus' words, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11).
How to pray Psalm 23
Read it slowly, one line a day, for a week, and notice which line meets you where you are.
Make it a conversation. "You are my shepherd. Today I'm lacking peace. Lead me to still waters."
Name your valley. When you reach verse 4, tell God what valley you're walking through, and thank Him that He's with you there.
Frequently asked questions
What is the main message of Psalm 23?
The main message of Psalm 23 is that God personally cares for His people like a good shepherd: He provides, gives rest, guides, protects, and stays with them even through the darkest valleys, and His goodness and love follow them all their lives.
What does "the valley of the shadow of death" mean?
It describes the darkest, most dangerous places in life, including grief, danger, and death itself. The psalm's comfort is not that the valley disappears, but that God is present in it: "I will fear no evil, for you are with me."
Who wrote Psalm 23?
Psalm 23 is titled "A Psalm by David." David had been a shepherd in his youth before becoming king of Israel, which gives the shepherd imagery a personal depth.
